# Framework Library

Reference for `gen-e2-strategy-frameworks`. Load the entry for the framework the decision table selected — do not read end to end.

### 1. Lean Canvas
**Created by:** Ash Maurya
**Best for:** Stage 0 — blank slate, or any time the business model is unclear

**What it does:**
A one-page view of the entire product and business model across nine boxes: Problem, Customer Segments, Unique Value Proposition, Solution, Channels, Revenue Streams, Cost Structure, Key Metrics, and Unfair Advantage. Any box that cannot be filled is a gap. It is the fastest way to expose what the team is assuming versus what they actually know.

**Key questions it answers:**
- What problem are we solving, and for whom?
- What is our unique value proposition?
- How will we reach customers and generate revenue?
- What does success look like, and how will we measure it?

**What gaps it typically surfaces:**
- Undefined target users or customer segments
- Unvalidated problem statements
- Missing revenue or business model clarity
- No defined success metrics

**Research activities it leads to:**
- Stakeholder interviews (to validate the problem and business model)
- Customer discovery interviews (to validate the customer segment and problem)
- Competitor analysis (to validate the unique value proposition)

**Apply when:** The project overview has multiple TBD sections and the team has not yet aligned on the core problem or business model.

### 2. Assumption Mapping
**Best for:** Cross-cutting — callable at any stage, any time the team is operating on beliefs rather than evidence

**What it does:**
Lists every belief the team holds about the product — users, problem, solution, market, technology — and plots each on a two-axis grid: how risky is this assumption if wrong (high/low) and how much evidence do we have to support it (known/unknown). High-risk, low-evidence assumptions are the most dangerous and must be researched first.

**Key questions it answers:**
- What are we taking for granted that we haven't validated?
- Which assumptions, if wrong, would kill the product?
- Where should we spend our research effort first?

**What gaps it typically surfaces:**
- Assumptions about user behaviour or motivation that have no research backing
- Technical or regulatory assumptions stated as facts
- Business model assumptions with no market evidence

**Research activities it leads to:**
- Prioritised research plan targeting the riskiest assumptions first
- User interviews, surveys, or desk research matched to each assumption type

**Apply when:** Starting any new project, or when the RAID log contains a high volume of `assumption`-tagged entries with no corresponding research activities planned.

### 3. SWOT Analysis
**Best for:** Stage 1 — problem defined but business context is thin

**What it does:**
Assesses the project across four dimensions: Strengths (what the client does well), Weaknesses (internal limitations), Opportunities (external factors that could help), and Threats (external factors that could hurt). Surfaces the business context needed to frame a realistic product strategy.

**Key questions it answers:**
- What does the client already have going for them?
- What internal constraints or limitations should shape the strategy?
- What market opportunities is this product trying to capture?
- What competitive or regulatory threats exist?

**What gaps it typically surfaces:**
- Missing competitive context
- Unacknowledged internal constraints (budget, capability, culture)
- Unclear market opportunity or timing

**Research activities it leads to:**
- Competitive analysis
- Stakeholder interviews (to surface internal strengths and weaknesses)
- Desk research (to assess market opportunities and threats)

**Apply when:** The project overview sections for Business Context, Known Constraints, and Ecosystem are TBD or thin.

### 4. PESTLE Analysis
**Best for:** Stage 1 — particularly for regulated industries, new markets, or products with external dependencies

**What it does:**
Scans the macro environment across six dimensions: Political, Economic, Social, Technological, Legal, and Environmental. Surfaces external factors the client may not have considered that could affect the product strategy.

**Key questions it answers:**
- What regulatory or policy environment is this product operating within?
- What economic conditions affect the market or the client's budget?
- What social or behavioural trends are relevant to the user base?
- What technology shifts are creating or closing windows of opportunity?

**What gaps it typically surfaces:**
- Unknown regulatory requirements
- External market forces not captured in the client brief
- Technology dependencies or constraints not yet identified

**Research activities it leads to:**
- Compliance and regulatory desk research
- Legal or compliance SME interviews
- Market and industry trend research

**Apply when:** The Regulatory section of the project overview is TBD, or the project operates in a regulated or politically sensitive environment.

### 5. Jobs To Be Done (JTBD)
**Created by:** Clayton Christensen / Anthony Ulwick
**Best for:** Stage 2 — context established but users are not understood

**What it does:**
Reframes the research question from "what do users want?" to "what job are users hiring this product to do?" A "job" is the progress a person is trying to make in a specific situation — the functional task, the emotional desire, and the social context around it. Understanding the job prevents building features nobody cares about.

**Key questions it answers:**
- What is the user actually trying to accomplish?
- What does success feel like to them, beyond task completion?
- What do they use today to get this job done, and where does it fall short?
- What would make them switch to a new solution?

**What gaps it typically surfaces:**
- Surface-level user descriptions with no understanding of motivation
- Feature ideas untethered to actual user needs
- Missing insight into what the user currently does instead (the real competition)

**Research activities it leads to:**
- JTBD user interviews (structured around the "switch" — when did they look for a new solution and why?)
- Contextual inquiry (observing users doing the job in their actual environment)

**Apply when:** The Target Demographic section has provisional or thin user descriptions, or personas exist but lack motivation and behavioural context.

### 6. User Story Mapping
**Created by:** Jeff Patton
**Best for:** Stage 3 — users understood but the end-to-end experience is not mapped

**What it does:**
Maps the user's journey across the horizontal axis (activities and tasks in sequence) and depth on the vertical axis (details and variations within each task). Makes the full scope of the user experience visible and reveals which parts are well understood versus unknown.

**Key questions it answers:**
- What does the user do from start to finish?
- Where are the gaps in our understanding of their experience?
- Which parts of the journey are most painful or most critical?
- What is the minimum experience needed to deliver real value?

**What gaps it typically surfaces:**
- Unmapped stages in the user journey
- Unknown edge cases or failure states
- Disconnects between what users need and what the proposed solution covers

**Research activities it leads to:**
- User interviews or contextual inquiry (to fill in unknown journey stages)
- Journey mapping workshops
- Service blueprint mapping (when backstage processes are also unclear)

**Apply when:** The team has user research but has not yet mapped the end-to-end experience, or the Features & Roadmap section is being populated without a shared view of the user journey.

### 7. Opportunity Solution Tree
**Created by:** Teresa Torres
**Best for:** Stage 3 — users understood, solution direction not yet defined

> **Nests with the Opportunity Canvas (#8):** the Opportunity Solution Tree maps the whole landscape of opportunities; the Opportunity Canvas then deep-dives the single opportunity you choose from that tree. Run the tree first to decide where to focus, then the canvas to go deep.

**What it does:**
A visual framework structured as: Desired Outcome → Opportunities (unmet user needs) → Solutions (ideas that address each opportunity) → Experiments (how to test each solution). Forces the team to stay grounded in user-validated opportunities before jumping to solutions, and ensures multiple solution directions are considered before committing to one.

**Key questions it answers:**
- What outcome are we trying to achieve?
- What opportunities (unmet needs) did research surface?
- What are the possible solutions for each opportunity?
- How do we test our assumptions before building?

**What gaps it typically surfaces:**
- Premature commitment to a single solution before exploring alternatives
- Solutions that cannot be traced back to a validated user opportunity
- Missing experiments to validate solution assumptions

**Research activities it leads to:**
- Concept testing and prototype interviews
- Experiment design (to validate solution assumptions before build)
- Prioritisation workshops (to select which opportunities to pursue first)

**Apply when:** Research is complete and the team needs to move from "we understand the problem" to "we have a direction for the solution."

### 8. Opportunity Canvas
**Created by:** Jeff Patton
**Best for:** Stage 3 — deep-diving a single opportunity chosen from the Opportunity Solution Tree

> **Nests under the Opportunity Solution Tree (#7):** use the tree to map and choose the opportunity, then use this canvas to pressure-test that one opportunity in depth before design begins.

**What it does:**
A one-page tool for exploring a single opportunity — covering the user need, the proposed solution, the business impact, how success will be measured, and the risks and assumptions involved. Useful for scoping a specific feature area or validating a proposed direction before investing in detailed design.

**Key questions it answers:**
- What user need or problem does this address?
- What is the proposed solution and how will users experience it?
- What is the business value of solving this?
- How will we know it worked?
- What are we assuming that could be wrong?

**What gaps it typically surfaces:**
- Undefined success criteria for a specific feature or solution
- Unvalidated assumptions underlying a proposed direction
- Missing business case for a proposed feature

**Research activities it leads to:**
- Usability testing or concept validation interviews
- Analytics review (to validate the scale of the problem)
- Stakeholder interviews (to confirm the business value)

**Apply when:** The team has a proposed solution direction and needs to pressure-test it before committing to design and build.

### 9. Value Proposition Canvas
**Created by:** Alexander Osterwalder
**Best for:** Stage 4 — solution direction exists, needs to be validated against user needs

**What it does:**
Maps the relationship between what the product offers (features, pain relievers, gain creators) and what users actually need (their jobs, pains, and gains). A strong value proposition is one where the product's offering directly addresses validated user needs — not assumed ones. Gaps between the two sides reveal misalignment.

**Key questions it answers:**
- Does our proposed solution actually relieve the user's most important pains?
- Does it help users achieve the gains they care most about?
- Are we building features that map to no real user need?

**What gaps it typically surfaces:**
- Features proposed for internal reasons rather than user need
- User pains or gains that the proposed solution doesn't address
- Missing differentiation — the solution solves the job but no better than what exists

**Research activities it leads to:**
- Validation interviews (presenting the value proposition to users and testing their reaction)
- Usability testing of early prototypes
- Competitive analysis (to validate differentiation)

**Apply when:** Research is complete and the team is defining the solution — use this to validate that what is being proposed actually matches what was learned.

### 10. Competitive Analysis / Benchmarking
**Best for:** Cross-cutting — callable at any stage, useful at multiple points throughout discovery

**What it does:**
Systematically reviews competitor products and market alternatives to understand what exists, what works, what fails, and where gaps remain. Informs positioning, differentiates the product, and prevents the team from building what already exists.

**Key questions it answers:**
- Who else is solving this problem, and how?
- Where are competitors strong, and where do they fall short?
- What do users already use as a workaround, and what do they dislike about it?
- Where is the genuine gap we can own?

**What gaps it typically surfaces:**
- Unknown or underestimated competitors
- Features being proposed that already exist and are well-solved
- Market positioning that is not differentiated

**Research activities it leads to:**
- Desk research and product teardowns
- Heuristic evaluation of competitor interfaces (`.gen-e2.he`)
- User interviews that include questions about current alternatives

**Apply when:** The Is / Is Not or Business Context sections are thin, or the team is making feature decisions without a clear view of the competitive landscape.

### 11. Kano Model
**Created by:** Noriaki Kano
**Best for:** Stage 5 — solution validated, MVP scope needs to be defined

**What it does:**
Categorises features into three types based on how they affect user satisfaction: Must-haves (basic expectations — their absence causes dissatisfaction), Performance features (the more you have, the more satisfied users are), and Delighters (unexpected features that create delight but whose absence isn't noticed). Helps the team ruthlessly prioritise what belongs in the MVP.

**Key questions it answers:**
- Which features are non-negotiable for the product to be acceptable?
- Which features will drive the most satisfaction if we invest in them?
- Which features are "nice to have" and can be deferred?

**What gaps it typically surfaces:**
- Delighters being prioritised over must-haves
- Performance features being scoped without understanding the baseline expectations
- MVP scope that does not meet the minimum threshold of user acceptability

**Research activities it leads to:**
- Kano surveys (presenting feature options to users and asking two questions: how do you feel if this feature is present? how do you feel if it is absent?)
- Prioritisation workshops with the client

**Apply when:** The Features & Roadmap section is being finalised and the team needs to make evidence-based decisions about what is in and out of the MVP.

### 12. RICE Scoring
**Created by:** Intercom
**Best for:** Stage 5 — alongside or after Kano, to rank and sequence features

**What it does:**
Scores each feature or initiative across four dimensions: Reach (how many users are affected), Impact (how much it moves the needle), Confidence (how sure are we of our estimates), and Effort (how much work is involved). Produces a ranked list that removes subjective debate from prioritisation.

**Key questions it answers:**
- Which features will have the highest return relative to the effort required?
- Where are we overconfident — and where should we do more research before committing?
- How should we sequence the roadmap to maximise early value?

**What gaps it typically surfaces:**
- High-effort, low-impact features being prioritised for the wrong reasons
- Low-confidence scores that reveal assumptions needing validation before scoping
- Scope decisions being made without a shared view of effort

**Research activities it leads to:**
- Technical scoping (to improve effort estimates)
- Additional user research (to improve confidence scores for uncertain features)

**Apply when:** The team has a feature list and needs to sequence the roadmap — use RICE after Kano has established what belongs in the MVP at all.

### 13. Feature Audit
**Source:** Intercom on Product Management
**Best for:** Lifecycle overlay — a conditional step for existing products, prepended before Stage 1 framing (not a stage in its own right)

**What it does:**
Maps every significant feature of an existing product on a two-axis grid: **frequency of use** (how often is this feature used?) on one axis and **breadth of adoption** (how many users use this feature?) on the other. The resulting quadrant reveals four categories of feature, each with a prescribed response:

| Position | What it means | Response |
|---|---|---|
| High frequency + high adoption | Core — this feature is working | Preserve and protect it |
| Low frequency + high adoption | Broadly available but rarely used — often misunderstood or seasonal | Increase frequency: improve discoverability, reduce friction |
| High frequency + low adoption | Power feature — loved by a niche | Increase adoption: onboard more users to it |
| Low frequency + low adoption | Not working | Kill it or redesign from scratch |

**Key questions it answers:**
- Which features are genuinely being used, and by how many people?
- Which features look used but are relied on only by a narrow segment?
- Where is investment going into features nobody uses?
- What should be cut, preserved, improved, or more actively promoted?

**What gaps it typically surfaces:**
- Features being maintained but not used — candidates for removal
- Features used by power users but invisible to the majority — candidates for onboarding investment
- Missing usage analytics needed to position features on the grid

**Research activities it leads to:**
- Analytics deep-dive (to gather frequency and adoption data)
- User interviews (to understand why low-adoption features are avoided)
- Competitor benchmarking (to validate whether dropped features are worth reimagining)

**Apply when:** The client has an existing product and the discovery scope includes evaluating the current feature set — not just defining new ones. Always run this before defining what to build next on an existing product.

### 14. Opportunity Algorithm
**Created by:** Anthony Ulwick
**Best for:** Stage 4–5 — when survey or interview data exists and the team needs a quantitative basis for prioritisation decisions

**What it does:**
Provides a mathematical method for identifying which unmet user outcomes represent the highest opportunity for investment. The algorithm answers a question that qualitative research alone cannot: not just *which outcomes are important* but *which important outcomes are currently underserved*?

**The formula:**
```
Opportunity Score = Importance + max(Importance − Satisfaction, 0)
```

Where:
- **Importance** = how important is this outcome to the user? (rated 1–10 in a survey)
- **Satisfaction** = how satisfied are users with how current solutions deliver this outcome? (rated 1–10)
- The `max(..., 0)` term ensures that outcomes where satisfaction exceeds importance do not reduce the score — over-served outcomes are neutralised, not penalised

**Interpreting scores:**
- Score ≥ 15: high opportunity — significantly underserved, strong candidate for investment
- Score 12–14: moderate opportunity — worth addressing
- Score < 10: low opportunity — either already well-served or not important enough to act on

**The five-step process:**
1. **Identify outcomes** — through JTBD or outcome-based interviews, capture the outcomes users are trying to achieve (not the features they want)
2. **Survey for importance** — ask users to rate the importance of each outcome (1 = not important, 10 = critically important)
3. **Survey for satisfaction** — ask the same users to rate how satisfied they are with how existing solutions deliver each outcome
4. **Calculate opportunity scores** — apply the formula to rank outcomes by opportunity
5. **Prioritise investment** — focus on the highest-scoring outcomes first; these are the areas where users care most and current solutions fail most

**Key questions it answers:**
- Which user outcomes are critically important but poorly served by current solutions?
- Where are we over-investing in outcomes users are already satisfied with?
- What is the evidence-based priority order for the feature roadmap?

**What gaps it typically surfaces:**
- Outcomes rated as high-importance in interviews but already well-served — deprioritise these
- Outcomes assumed to be important that users rate as low-importance — challenge and potentially remove from scope
- Missing quantitative data — if importance and satisfaction scores do not exist, this is a research gap requiring a structured survey before the algorithm can be applied

**Research activities it leads to:**
- Outcome-based survey design (to collect importance and satisfaction ratings)
- Scoring and analysis workshop (to apply the formula and review results with the client)

**Apply when:** The team has completed user research and is preparing the feature roadmap. Use the Opportunity Algorithm before RICE to ensure prioritisation is grounded in validated importance, not assumption or stakeholder volume.

> **Note on data requirements:** The Opportunity Algorithm requires survey-rated data, not just interview impressions. If structured importance and satisfaction ratings do not exist, log this as a research gap in RAID and plan a quantitative survey before applying the formula. Do not estimate scores from qualitative impressions — the method depends on real user ratings.

### 15. Ten-Question Feature Checklist
**Source:** Intercom on Product Management
**Best for:** Stage 5 — as a rapid pre-prioritisation filter run first, before applying Kano, RICE, or the Opportunity Algorithm

**What it does:**
A rapid acid-test for any proposed feature. Before investing in scoping, design, or delivery, each feature should be able to answer yes — or a qualified, evidence-backed yes — to these ten questions. Features that fail multiple questions are candidates for rejection or significant rework before they enter the roadmap.

**The ten questions:**
1. Does it fit our product vision?
2. Will it still matter in five years?
3. Will a large majority of our users benefit from it?
4. Does it deepen the core value our product delivers — rather than just adding surface variety?
5. Will it grow the business, directly through revenue or indirectly through retention and acquisition?
6. Can we build it well? Do we have the capability and capacity to do it properly?
7. Does it improve or maintain the product's simplicity, or does it increase cognitive load?
8. Is this the right time to build it — given current priorities and technical readiness?
9. Is there a validated user need driving it — or is it a solution in search of a problem?
10. Have we heard this need from multiple independent sources — or is it based on a single loud voice?

**How to use it:**
Work through the checklist for each proposed feature before it enters prioritisation. A feature that fails questions 1, 3, 5, or 9 should be challenged explicitly before proceeding. A feature that fails three or more questions should be flagged for removal or placed in a parked backlog pending further validation.

**What gaps it typically surfaces:**
- Features driven by a single customer request rather than a pattern of validated need
- Features that add short-term revenue but dilute the product vision over time
- Features that assume capability the team does not yet have
- Features that solve the wrong problem because they were proposed rather than derived from research

**Apply when:** Before any feature enters the prioritisation process — run this checklist first. It is a lightweight gate that prevents weak features from consuming prioritisation effort downstream.
